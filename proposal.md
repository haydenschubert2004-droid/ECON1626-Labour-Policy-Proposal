# Helping Australia's General Clerks Adapt to Generative AI

**Client and context:** Advice to the Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR) on implementing Action 5, “Support and train Australians”, of the National AI Plan (DISR, 2025).

## Executive summary

DEWR should commission a two-year comparative trial capped at $1.265 million: up to 500 general clerks offered assessed training assistance and 500 offered that assistance plus co-funded paid release and workplace support. Clerical tasks face automation exposure, but this does not establish inevitable redundancies or a universal training deficit. Eligibility should require a demonstrated skills gap, changing tasks and worker-confirmed barriers. Existing free provision should be used first. Evidence from earlier training programmes supports practical learning while warning that subsidies can fund activity already planned. The trial should therefore test additional workplace benefits, not count enrolments as success. Employers should share costs and consult workers. Expansion should depend on improved capability and workplace performance relative to training assistance, acceptable incremental costs and no material job-quality harm.

## Problem and economic rationale

TAFE NSW offers free applied AI content; Future Skills Organisation provides employer resources (TAFE NSW, n.d.; FSO, n.d.). The unresolved question is who needs additional support to use these resources effectively.

JSA (2025a, p. 20) identifies elevated automation potential in middle-skill occupations, including clerical work. ABS (2026) finds time/workload was the main barrier for 44% of people reporting training barriers; only 4.6% of people aged 15–74 reported such barriers. These findings justify investigating exposed workers, not subsidising the entire occupation.

Transferable learning benefits future employers as well as the firm paying today, potentially causing underinvestment. Workers may also lack information about suitable training or time to participate. Conversely, employers capture productivity gains and should finance ordinary business improvements. Subsidise additional portable skills, with co-funding and verified need, rather than software purchases or routine induction.

## Labour-market impacts

Consider a health-service clerk: AI could draft correspondence while the worker verifies details, resolves booking exceptions and explains procedures. Time saved before checking errors is not net productivity. Training should combine tool use with judgement and communication.

Automation reduces labour needed per unit of output; lower costs, increased demand and new tasks can offset displacement (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2019). In budget-constrained services, gains may reduce backlogs rather than expand staffing. Entry-level opportunities may contract when routine learning tasks disappear.

DEWR (2026, pp. 44–57) finds a modest negative exposure–employment relationship after ChatGPT's introduction, sensitive to modelling choices and without observing firm adoption. This is not proof of AI-caused redundancies.

Workers with complementary skills may gain bargaining power; workers doing substitutable tasks may face weaker demand. Even successful training cannot guarantee wage growth where few employers offer alternatives. Consultation over workload and task allocation therefore complements portable skills. Assess social value using output quality, time genuinely saved and worker wellbeing, against training time and delivery resources. Wage gains and employer profits may divide the same productivity benefit; counting both independently would overstate the return.

JSA (2025b) finds larger firms adopting faster. Integration costs and proprietary data could widen productivity gaps and shift market share towards capable firms, although affordable tools also lower entry costs. Vendor-neutral skills reduce dependence on one employer or platform.

General clerks numbered 292,100 in February 2026; women represented 81% and part-time workers 40% in 2025 averages (JSA, n.d., ANZSCO profile). Employer-led access could exclude workers with irregular hours or weak bargaining power. Flexible scheduling and worker nominations should counter this; demographic shares alone do not establish individual need.

## Policy options and evidence

Compare three two-year options for up to 1,000 eligible clerks. Standalone ceilings are proposed design limits, not supplier quotations.

| Option and service | Efficiency | Equity | Feasibility | Public ceiling |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Assessed learning assistance, up to $500 each | Portable skills; fees may not be the binding barrier | Worker choice; unpaid discretionary time remains unequal | Existing providers; verify additional need | $0.935m |
| 2. Learning plus paid release and workplace support | Addresses application; risks subsidising existing plans | Paid access; dependent on employer cooperation | Payroll checks and flexible group delivery | $1.595m |
| 3. Counselling, matching and bridging learning after documented role loss/change, up to $1,500 each | Supports mobility where redeployment fails | Reaches displaced workers | Coordinate existing services; excludes full qualifications | $2.035m |

Each ceiling includes $350,000 administration/evaluation and 10% contingency. Option 2 adds $600 direct support per participant to option 1.

Evidence does not establish a universally superior instrument. Abramovsky et al. (2011) found no significant training-uptake effect from UK employer-training pilots using difference-in-differences. Fleuren et al.'s (2020) experiment with 230 Dutch insurance employees favoured flexible time-or-money vouchers over fixed offers. Canada's UPSKILL experiment improved skills and job performance, but release-time constraints persisted (Gyarmati et al., 2014). These different settings inform design, not Australian AI effect estimates.

Prefer option 1 when fees bind and option 3 when viable internal roles disappear. Test option 2 where workers confirm time/application barriers, allowing choice of suitable learning and scheduling.

## Recommended trial and implementation

Require a baseline skills assessment and worker-confirmed task-change plan. Fund only gaps not met by existing services. Both trial groups retain ordinary services and paid-training entitlements.

All budget figures are proposed Australian-dollar spending ceilings.

The maximum trial budget is:

- Learning: 1,000 × up to $500 = $500,000.
- Paid release: 500 × 20 hours × 50% × $40 = $200,000.
- Workplace facilitation: 50 ten-person cohorts × up to $2,000 = $100,000.
- Administration: $150,000; independent evaluation: $200,000.
- Contingency: 10% of $1.15 million = $115,000.

The $40 wage benchmark is JSA's May 2025 median hourly earnings. Reimburse half actual eligible wages, capped at $20 hourly; employers pay the balance and on-costs. UPSKILL also compensated half release time, supporting this design precedent rather than its optimality. Job-related training already requires pay (FWO, n.d.).

TAFE's free content lasts 3–5 hours. The 20-hour allowance adds supervised practice and assessment; cohort facilitation covers task mapping and feedback. All service allowances require competitive pricing. The $500 is an authorisation limit, not an automatic payment: providers must itemise additional instruction and assessment. Unused allowances return to DEWR. Varying direct costs by 25%, holding overhead fixed, gives $1.045–$1.485 million: redesign within the ceiling.

DEWR should commission delivery; providers teach, employers release staff, and JSA advises on targeting. Months 1–3 validate needs, prices and evaluation feasibility; 4–6 recruit and assess baselines; 7–12 deliver; 13–24 evaluate and publish a decision.

Independently randomise employers between offers after fixing participant lists, balancing baseline capability and keeping each group within 500 workers. Confirm sufficient employers for credible inference before launch.

At six months, assess capability using a standardised task, a proposed 90% accuracy threshold and no critical factual/privacy errors. Separately measure workplace processing time, rework, earnings, hours and workload. Analyse original allocation, baseline differences and attrition; report gender and employment-type outcomes. Simulated performance alone cannot establish workplace productivity.

At full spending, workplace support adds $300,000 before contingency. Illustratively, 50 additional successful learners imply $6,000 each, excluding common overhead and contingency—not a forecast. DEWR should specify an acceptable incremental cost before results. Expand only with credible workplace benefits and no material job-quality deterioration.

## Risks and mitigations

Audit baseline training plans and payroll to limit deadweight. Recruit through worker organisations, provide flexible sessions and record employer refusals. Use non-sensitive practice data, human verification and vendor-neutral materials. Record independently purchased support in both groups. Stop or redesign if unmet need, affordable delivery or reliable evaluation cannot be established.
