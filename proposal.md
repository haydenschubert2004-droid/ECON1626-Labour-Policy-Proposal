# Helping Australia's Clerical Workforce Adapt to Generative AI

- **Client and policy context:** Advice to the Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR) on implementing Action 5, "Support and train Australians", of the National AI Plan.
- **Status:** Evidence-checked working bullet-point outline, revised 25 September 2026; not the final 1,200-word proposal.
- **Central question:** What additional support would best help clerical and administrative workers adapt to generative AI while improving productivity and limiting unequal adjustment costs?
- **Working scope:** General clerks with documented AI-related task change and an unmet learning need. Do not restrict eligibility to small businesses without stronger evidence.
- **Working position:** Test targeted support for paid learning time and practical job redesign against worker-directed training assistance and career-transition support. Option 2 is a provisional trial recommendation; its benefit over existing provision remains to be tested. See [research notes](#working-research-and-design-notes) for evidence limits and costing assumptions.

## Executive summary — final target 130 words

- Identify the problem as unequal capacity to adapt to changing tasks, rather than assume economy-wide technological unemployment.
- Summarise the economic case for intervention and the group facing the clearest demonstrated barriers.
- Compare three distinct instruments using efficiency, equity, feasibility and fiscal cost.
- Present a bounded, costed recommendation with delivery responsibilities and measurable outcomes once the comparison is complete.

## Problem definition and current policy — final target 150 words

- Anchor the advice in Action 5 of the National AI Plan, which addresses workforce skills and adaptation [1].
- Establish the existing baseline: government announced one million fully subsidised introductory AI course places in December 2025 [2]. This is an offer of places, not evidence of one million completions.
- Existing provision includes free TAFE NSW applied AI content, SEE workplace foundation-skills support and Future Skills Organisation employer resources. Fund a demonstrated gap rather than duplicate these services; source details are in the [evidence register](#evidence-register).
- ABS finds time/workload is the main barrier for 44% of people reporting work-related training barriers, not 44% of all workers. This supports testing time constraints but does not establish their prevalence among general clerks.
- Examine possible economic rationales: workers' financial constraints, employers' limited incentive to fund transferable skills, information gaps and coordination costs. Treat these as hypotheses requiring evidence in the selected group.
- Do not describe a completed consultation as open or claim that no support currently exists.

## Economic and distributional analysis — final target 300 words

- **Tasks:** Use Jobs and Skills Australia's distinction between automation and augmentation [3]. Illustrative tasks to assess include routine document preparation, record processing and correspondence; contrast these with verification, judgement and interpersonal work.
- **Employment:** Explain how lower labour requirements per unit of output can displace tasks, while lower costs, increased demand and new complementary tasks can offset employment losses.
- **Observed outcomes:** DEWR reports suggestive slowing in some highly exposed occupations, but not evidence of broad AI-driven labour-market upheaval [4]. Do not infer causation from exposure alone.
- **Wages:** Examine changes in demand for routine and complementary skills, workers' outside options and bargaining power. Higher productivity need not produce equal wage gains.
- **Industry structure:** Assess whether AI lowers entry costs or instead advantages firms with stronger data, scale and implementation capacity. Distinguish these possibilities from demonstrated concentration.
- **Distribution:** Use occupational and demographic data to assess differences by gender, age, employment type and region. Choose the most relevant contrasts rather than list every group.
- **Occupational context:** JSA's ANZSCO general-clerk profile reports 292,100 employed (February 2026), 81% women and 40% part-time (2025 averages). Keep reference periods explicit; these figures describe the occupation, not eligible demand. Address the transition to OSCA classifications before implementation.
- Use task-based economic research to support mechanisms, without treating overseas findings as Australian effect estimates [5].

## Three policy options and evaluation — final target 240 words

- Compare all options against existing provision, rather than against a fictional absence of policy.
- **Option 1 — Worker-directed applied training assistance:** Fund a skills assessment and relevant training beyond already-free introductory provision. Target workers with a demonstrated skills gap; examine worker choice and portability against course quality, access and completion risks.
- **Option 2 — Employer co-funded paid learning and job redesign:** Reimburse part of verified paid learning time and applied workplace support. Target employers with a credible plan for changing clerical tasks; examine practical uptake against employer capture, administration and subsidising activity that would happen anyway.
- **Option 3 — Career-transition assistance:** Provide targeted counselling, recognition of existing skills, job matching and bridging training for workers facing documented role change or displacement. Examine mobility benefits against uncertain vacancies and the cost of intervening after disruption.
- Apply the same criteria to each: additional productivity/employment benefits; distribution of benefits and access; delivery feasibility; and incremental public expenditure.
- Compare costs over 24 months using participants × additional service costs plus administration and evaluation. Benchmark options 1 and 3 before assigning dollar totals; unlike participant needs prevent a simple cost-per-head ranking.
- Use a concise options-by-criteria matrix in the final draft; justify judgements and avoid unsupported numerical scores.

## Recommended package, costing and implementation — final target 280 words

- **Provisional candidate:** A limited test of option 2 using existing suitable training, conditional on evidence that paid time and workplace application are important unmet barriers.
- Select another option, combine justified components or retain existing arrangements if additional benefits do not support the candidate.
- **Eligibility:** General clerks with documented task change and unmet transferable-skill needs; allow part-time and casual access. Exclude routine induction/compliance and services already funded. Employers must already pay employees for job-related training: the proposal subsidises additional investment, not a new wage entitlement.
- **Proposed roles:** DEWR sponsors and commissions delivery; JSA informs targeting and evaluation; training providers deliver learning; employers provide paid time and implement agreed task changes. Verify mandates and available delivery channels.
- **Illustrative schedule:** Months 1–3 for needs assessment, pricing and evaluation design; 4–6 for recruitment/baselines; 7–12 for delivery; 13–24 for follow-up and a published decision. Confirm authority, data access and feasible evaluation sample before launch.
- **Cost method:** Participants × supported learning hours × reimbursed hourly cost, plus any additional course fees, workplace advice, administration and evaluation. Separate public subsidy, employer contribution and workers' time.
- **Provisional ceiling:** Up to 1,000 workers; 20 paid hours; 50% wage reimbursement with the eligible hourly wage capped at $40 ($400,000). Add needs-based learning up to $500,000, distinct workplace support $200,000, administration $150,000, evaluation $200,000 and 10% contingency: $1.595 million. These are design allowances, not verified market prices; the [costing notes](#provisional-option-2-budget-a-ceiling-not-a-quotation) explain assumptions and sensitivity. Free content attracts no tuition subsidy.
- **KPIs:** Completion and demonstrated skill use; employment and earnings at follow-up; job quality/workload; employer adoption; participation across priority groups; cost per additional successful outcome.
- Prefer randomised phased offers at employer level, subject to feasibility and a power calculation. Compare additional participation and assessed skill application against existing provision; monitor employment, earnings and job quality without promising detectable short-run effects.
- Pre-specify criteria for expansion, redesign or closure. Do not count the same productivity benefit twice as both wage gains and business gains.

## Risks and mitigations — final target 100 words

- Subsidising existing activity: assess additional need, require evidence of delivery and audit claims.
- Excluding workers with weaker employer support: accessible recruitment, paid participation and a route to worker-directed assistance.
- Low-quality or vendor-specific training: assess transferable skills and practical application.
- Training without suitable work: connect learning to real task changes or credible vacancies.
- Overstated AI effects: distinguish forecasts, exposure measures and observed causal evidence.
- Excessive complexity: use a small number of transparent eligibility rules and existing delivery systems where suitable.

## Working research and design notes

Working research record, 25 September 2026. These notes are retained here during drafting and will be condensed into the final 1,200-word brief; Git history will preserve this working version. Verified descriptions, economic interpretation and proposed design assumptions are separated below.

### Research question and decision

Should DEWR supplement existing AI skills provision with a targeted trial of paid learning time and workplace application for general clerks under Action 5 of the National AI Plan?

Recommend developing a bounded trial, not asserting that a national subsidy already has proven benefits. General clerks provide a manageable occupational scope; restrict access by demonstrated task change and unmet need rather than assume every exposed worker needs assistance. There is not yet sufficient evidence here to restrict eligibility to small businesses.

### Economic case to test

- Transferable skills can benefit future employers as well as the firm paying for training, potentially weakening employers' investment incentives.
- Time, information and coordination constraints can prevent useful training even when content is free.
- Neither mechanism establishes the size of underinvestment among general clerks. Screen for unmet need and measure additional participation.
- Productivity may increase without wage gains if bargaining power or outside options are weak. Include worker consultation, job quality and earnings in evaluation.
- AI could lower entry costs while data, complementary investment and vendor scale favour larger firms. Treat industrial concentration as a possible mechanism, not an observed result proved by these sources.
- Female and part-time workforce shares justify examining access and outcomes; they do not establish that every woman or part-time worker is disadvantaged.

### Fair comparison of three options

Use existing free training and services as the baseline over a common 24-month horizon. Do not give unsupported numerical scores or claim cost-effectiveness without outcome estimates.

| Option | Mechanism and target | Main advantage | Main limitation | Transparent cost basis |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Worker-directed assistance | Needs assessment and capped additional applied learning for employed general clerks with a demonstrated gap. | Worker choice and portable skills. | Paying course fees may leave time and workplace barriers unresolved. | Eligible participants × actual additional learning cost, plus delivery and evaluation; exclude free provision. |
| 2. Co-funded paid learning and workplace application | Employers release eligible clerks for transferable learning and consult workers on task changes. | Addresses time and application together. | Deadweight, employer selection and administrative burden; benefits are unproven. | Wage reimbursement plus separately justified learning/support, administration and evaluation. |
| 3. Transition assistance | Counselling, skills recognition, matching and bridging learning for documented role loss/change. | Supports mobility and workers outside supportive employers. | Suitable vacancies matter; it may arrive after disruption. | Clients × assessed service intensity, plus administration/evaluation; avoid duplicating employment services. |

Evaluate efficiency, equity, feasibility and incremental fiscal cost consistently. The third option serves a different need; equal participant counts do not imply equivalent benefits. Previous illustrative alternative totals are not validated comparative prices and should not determine the ranking. Obtain service-cost benchmarks before the final options table.

### Provisional option 2 budget: a ceiling, not a quotation

All amounts are Australian dollars. Scale, hours, subsidy rate and allowances are author-designed assumptions. The $40 hourly benchmark comes from the occupational profile but is not a loaded employer cost, prescribed wage or universal worker rate.

| Component | Proposed maximum calculation | Public funding |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Paid learning reimbursement | 1,000 workers × up to 20 hours × 50% × eligible hourly wage capped at $40 | $400,000 |
| Additional assessed learning | Up to $500 per worker, only where existing free provision cannot meet the diagnosed need | $500,000 |
| Workplace support | Planning allowance of $200 per worker; scope separate from learning to avoid double payment | $200,000 |
| Administration | Unbenchmarked planning allowance | $150,000 |
| Independent evaluation | Unbenchmarked planning allowance | $200,000 |
| Subtotal | Maximum of the above | $1,450,000 |
| Contingency | 10% of subtotal | $145,000 |
| **Maximum illustrative envelope** | **Subject to cost validation and design approval** | **$1,595,000** |

The $500 allowance must not be described as a tuition fee payable for every participant: free applied content exists. Actual approved expenditure could be substantially below this envelope. Employers meet remaining wages and applicable on-costs; at the assumed $40 rate and full hours their base-wage contribution is $400,000. Higher wages increase employer contributions under this cap.

Sensitivity, holding administration/evaluation at $350,000: varying the $1.1 million direct-support allowance by ±25%, then applying 10% contingency, yields $1,292,500–$1,897,500. This is a design sensitivity range, not a confidence interval or permission to exceed the proposed ceiling. No return-on-investment estimate is established.

### Implementation and evaluation assumptions

- Months 1–3: DEWR commissions overlap/needs assessment, provider pricing, worker consultation and independent evaluation design. Confirm delivery authority and data access; JSA is a proposed analytical adviser, not an assumed programme operator.
- Months 4–6: recruit eligible workplaces and workers; collect baseline skills, existing training plans, wages, hours and job-quality measures. Specify the occupational crosswalk if recruitment uses OSCA.
- Months 7–12: deliver learning and supervised application using existing resources where suitable; verify payroll and avoid duplicate claims.
- Months 13–24: collect six- and twelve-month follow-up where timing permits; publish findings and decide whether to stop, redesign or expand.
- Prefer randomised phased offers at employer level if feasible. Both groups retain ordinary access to existing services and legal entitlements. Account for employer clustering and spillovers.
- Commission statistical power calculations before finalising scale; 1,000 workers is a budget assumption, not proof of sufficient power.
- Primary outcomes: additional participation and independently assessed skill application. Secondary outcomes: earnings, hours, retention, workload and task quality. Report access/outcomes by gender and employment type where samples permit.
- Pre-register outcomes, analysis and expansion rules before delivery. Course completion alone cannot justify expansion; seek improvement over the baseline with acceptable cost and no material job-quality harm. Set numerical thresholds through design and costing rather than invent them here.
